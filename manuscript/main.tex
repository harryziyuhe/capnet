\Shorttitle{\pkg{capnet}: Contribution-Capped Regularization}

\Abstract{
    \pkg{capnet} is an \proglang{R} package for generalized linear models (GLMs) with contribution-capped regularization. The method supplements the elastic net with a penalty on feature contributions that exceed user-specified ceilings. An evaluation matrix, which may differ from the training design matrix, identifies the covariate realizations on which these contributions are assessed. This formulation addresses settings in which extreme covariate values can dominate predictions despite coefficient shrinkage. We derive the penalty and its gradient, describe estimation and sequential prediction workflows, and illustrate the method in a simulation, a Gaussian market-timing application, and a Poisson web-traffic forecasting application. In these examples, contribution penalties can improve out-of-sample performance relative to uncapped and post-fit capped models. More broadly, the paper provides a worked recipe for incorporating application-specific penalties into regression: define the behavior to be discouraged, express it as a penalty, and integrate that penalty into estimation.
}

\section{Introduction}

In many predictive applications, model behavior depends on both coefficient magnitude and the realized contribution of each feature to the linear predictor. Extreme covariate values can allow a few features to dominate predictions, creating sensitivity to rare events or conflicting with preferences about how much a prediction should depend on any one signal. This issue is especially salient when covariates are skewed or heavy-tailed, or when models are evaluated in stress scenarios that are poorly represented in the training sample. Classical penalties such as ridge regression \citep{hoerl1970}, the lasso \citep{tibshirani1996}, and the elastic net \citep{zou2005} shrink coefficients, but they do not directly target the contributions realized at particular covariate values. Robust regression, response-distribution choices, and transformations address other sources of instability; their usual formulations do not specify how large an individual feature's contribution may be.

Quantitative finance provides one motivation. A return forecast may feed a position-sizing rule, so a tail observation in one predictor can make a downstream decision unusually dependent on that signal. Risk managers can impose position limits or exposure budgets, but it can also be useful to discourage signal dominance while estimating the forecasting model. Related concerns arise in credit scoring, epidemiology, and web or network traffic forecasting, where unusual covariate values may appear at prediction time and where the cost of an extreme forecast can differ from the cost of an ordinary error. The appropriate ceiling is application-specific: an extreme contribution may be useful information in one setting and an unwanted sensitivity in another.

This paper introduces \pkg{capnet}, a penalized regression framework and accompanying \proglang{R} package that supplements the elastic net with a penalty on large feature contributions. The elastic net combines the sparsity-inducing $\ell_1$ penalty with the $\ell_2$ penalty used in ridge regression \citep{zou2005}. Contribution caps add a separate target: the magnitude of $Z_{ij}\beta_j$, the contribution of feature $j$ at evaluation covariate row $i$. The evaluation matrix $Z$ may differ from the training design matrix. It can represent training observations, specified stress scenarios, or covariate values available when a forecast is made. A finite penalty makes ceilings soft rather than guaranteed bounds; the goal is to let estimation balance predictive fit against the cost of excessive contributions in the selected evaluation environment.

The formulation applies to generalized linear models (GLMs), where contributions enter the linear predictor but have different interpretations under different links. A contribution is additive for a Gaussian identity-link mean, whereas it changes log-odds in a logistic model or the log-mean in a Poisson model. The supported losses and contribution penalty yield a convex objective. The \pkg{capnet} package provides model fitting, cross-validation, contribution diagnostics, and sequential estimation, using quasi-Newton methods for unconstrained coefficient fits and a projected solver when coefficient bounds are supplied.

The contribution is conceptual as well as computational. Coefficient regularization controls parameter magnitude, while contribution regularization evaluates how the fitted coefficients behave at specified covariate values. Estimating with the contribution penalty can change several coefficients at once; clipping contributions after fitting cannot make that adjustment. We illustrate this distinction with a repeated simulation and two empirical applications. These examples examine prediction error and feature contributions, not universal robustness guarantees or the outcome of a specified portfolio strategy. The paper also serves as a worked recipe for expressing an application-specific modeling preference as a penalty and checking its empirical trade-off; other penalties would require their own derivations and validation.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:motivate} motivates contribution control and reviews related methods. Section~\ref{sec:framework} defines the objective and optimization procedure. Section~\ref{sec:software} presents the software interface and optimization strategy used in the \pkg{capnet} package. Section~\ref{sec:examples} reports the finance and web-traffic applications. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with a discussion of limitations and directions for future research.

\section{Motivations}\label{sec:motivate}

\subsection{Why Control Feature COntributions?}

Contribution caps are motivated by predictive settings in which a small number of features can exert disproportionate influence on fitted values, particularly when observations fall in the tails of the covariate distribution. These tail-region observations may be rare but substantively important, and they can induce unstable predictions when individual feature contributions become large. Consider a predictive task governed by the data-generating process where covariates $X_1, \ldots, X_p$ jointly determine the response $Y$.

Under the classical linear-model assumptions, including a full-rank design and exogenous, uncorrelated errors with constant variance, ordinary least squares (OLS) is the best linear unbiased estimator. Penalization is not required for that result, and a large realized contribution may represent genuine signal. In practice, multicollinearity, high-dimensionality, or model misspecification can motivate $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ regularization to stabilize estimation. The contribution penalty addresses a further, application-specific concern about model behavior at selected covariate values.

In many applications, covariates are skewed or exhibit unusually frequent extremes, so prediction may encounter observations in the tails of their distributions. These observations can be rare in the training data yet substantively important, generating abrupt spikes in realized feature contributions. Examples include periods of market stress in finance, extreme environmental conditions in climate modeling, and rare risk profiles in insurance or credit scoring. An extreme covariate realization can induce a large contribution $X_{ij}\beta_j$ even when its coefficient has been shrunk. A model may therefore appear well behaved on typical training rows but produce a prediction dominated by one feature on an evaluation row. Traditional coefficient regularization reduces this risk indirectly; it does not target a specified contribution ceiling for that row.

A substantial literature on fat-tailed data addresses related issues by modeling unusual response distributions. Approaches include response transformations, Gamma or inverse Gaussian models for positive responses, quantile regression, mixtures, and spliced distributions \citep{shi2014,gan2018}. These methods can improve modeling of the conditional distribution of $Y$. Their usual formulations do not specify a ceiling for an individual feature's contribution to the linear predictor, so a response-model choice alone need not prevent a large covariate realization from dominating a fitted value.

\subsection{Other Approaches to Extreme Covariates}

Practitioners have proposed a variety of strategies to mitigate the effects of heavy-tailed covariates in regression models. These approaches include preprocessing techniques such as transformations and winsorization, robust regression and leverage-based methods, and post-hoc modifications to fitted values. While each of these methods addresses certain aspects of instability induced by extreme covariate realizations, they do so indirectly and do not provide explicit control over realized feature contributions to the linear predictor.

Preprocessing techniques such as logarithmic, power, or rank-based transformations, as well as winsorization and trimming, are commonly used to compress the tails of covariate distributions prior to model fitting. These methods can reduce the influence of extreme covariate values and improve numerical stability. However, they require choices of transformation or truncation thresholds and can alter the scale and interpretation of covariates. A fixed rule can also be applied to new rows, but it does not jointly choose coefficients to balance predictive fit against feature-specific contribution ceilings.

Robust regression techniques, including residual-based M-estimators, reduce the influence of observations with large errors \citep{huber1973}. Residual downweighting alone need not address a high-leverage covariate row with a small residual, though leverage-aware methods can. Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) instead minimizes worst-case risk over a specified neighborhood of plausible data-generating distributions \citep{bental2009}. These approaches target observation-level influence or aggregate predictive risk. Their standard objectives do not directly regulate individual products $X_{ij}\beta_j$, although they can be adapted or combined with contribution control when the application calls for it.

Another strategy imposes limits on contributions or fitted values after model estimation. Post-fit clipping can enforce a hard limit on the quantities it clips, but the coefficients were estimated without that limit. It cannot adjust other coefficients to compensate for a clipped contribution. A contribution penalty instead allows coefficients to change jointly during estimation, while accepting that its ceilings are soft. The empirical comparisons below examine the predictive consequences of these distinct choices.

In addition to these methodological approaches, several software packages provide penalized or constrained generalized linear model fitting. The \pkg{glmnet} package \citep{friedman2010} implements efficient algorithms for $\ell_1$- and $\ell_2$-regularized GLMs, but regularization acts only on the coefficient vector and does not constrain realized feature contributions. Packages such as \pkg{mgcv} \citep{wood2017} impose smoothness or shape constraints on functional relationships, while bias-reduction methods such as \pkg{brglm2} \citep{kosmidis2019} address small-sample bias and separation issues. Although these tools provide valuable forms of regularization and stabilization, they do not offer mechanisms for directly bounding the influence of individual features.

Taken together, these approaches target different components of model instability: preprocessing changes covariates, robust regression changes the role of observations in estimation, and DRO addresses uncertainty about the data-generating distribution. Feature-level contribution control poses a different question: what cost should be assigned when a selected covariate value gives one feature an unusually large role in the prediction?

Contribution caps address this question by adding a penalty for large realized products $Z_{ij}\beta_j$ during optimization. The GLM loss still uses the training observations, while the evaluation matrix $Z$ identifies the covariate values at which contribution size matters. Setting $Z$ to selected stress scenarios or to covariates available at a forecast origin incorporates those considerations without fitting a separate coefficient vector for each regime. A finite penalty discourages exceedances; it does not guarantee a bound on $Z$, much less on covariate values absent from $Z$. The same coefficient vector applies across the selected scenarios, with its estimation reflecting the specified trade-off between fit and contribution size.

\subsection{Illustrative Simulation}

We use a repeated simulation to examine contribution control when observed covariates are noisy proxies for latent signals. Each of 10 independent replications has 400 training and 100 test observations with eight predictors. Latent rows follow $X_i^*\sim N(0,\Sigma)$, where $\Sigma_{jk}=0.5^{|j-k|}$, and
\[
y_i=X_i^{*\top}\beta+\varepsilon_i,\qquad
\beta=(2.5,1.9,1.3,0.9,0.8,0,0,0)^\top,\qquad
\varepsilon_i\sim N(0,1).
\]
The observed predictors are $X=X^*+E$.

The first five features carry signal and the last three have zero coefficients. In the mixture scenario, features 1 and 2 have proxy-noise standard deviation 10, with spike probability 0.10 in training and 0.20 in the test batch; otherwise their noise standard deviation is 0.25. The remaining features retain standard deviation 0.25. The homoskedastic control uses standard deviation 0.25 for every feature. The mixture is an explicit test-time stress shift: the evaluation batch contains more large proxy errors than the training sample. This model separates genuine signal from occasional large proxy errors. The composite error in a regression on observed predictors contains measurement error and is generally correlated with those predictors. The simulation therefore illustrates an application of contribution control, rather than a general correction for measurement-error bias. The variance-mixture noise is leptokurtic but is not heavy-tailed in the strict asymptotic sense.

Within each replication and scenario, ten-fold cross-validation selects the uncapped elastic net's $\lambda$ with $\alpha=0.5$ and standardized training columns. The selected $\lambda$ is held fixed across capped specifications. Let $b_j^{\rm uni}$ denote a univariate training OLS slope. We construct training-only contribution scales
\[
B_j=\max\left\{|b_j^{\rm uni}|q_{0.95}(|X_{\cdot j}|),\;0.05s_y\right\},
\qquad L_j=\kappa B_j,
\]
where the quantile and response standard deviation use training observations only. This calibration expresses ceilings in contribution units and avoids nearly zero ceilings for features with weak univariate associations. We cross $\kappa\in\{0.5,1,2,4\}$ with $\gamma\in\{0.1,1,10,100\}$. The baseline sets $\gamma=0$.

Joint evaluation fits one model using all test covariate rows in the penalty, assuming those covariates are available together. Walk-forward evaluation instead re-optimizes on each test row with the historical training sample held fixed. Neither scheme uses test responses in fitting or ceiling calibration. Tables~\ref{tab:simulate_full} and~\ref{tab:simulate_walk} report mean test MSE across the 10 mixture-scenario replications. The homoskedastic-control results and replication-level details are in \path{vignettes/paper/example_simulation.Rmd}.

\section{The Contribution-Cap Framework}\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Model Setup}

Let $y\in\mathbb{R}^n$ denote the response vector and $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ the training design matrix. Row $i$ is $X_i^\top$, and $\beta\in\mathbb{R}^p$ contains the slope coefficients. With intercept $\beta_0$, the linear predictor and conditional mean are
\begin{equation}
\eta_i=\beta_0+X_i^\top\beta,\qquad \mu_i=g^{-1}(\eta_i).
\label{eq:predictor}
\end{equation}
For Gaussian regression with identity link, we minimize the average squared-error loss, equivalent to a negative log-likelihood with unit variance up to a constant. The same estimation framework applies to other families with a smooth, convex loss in $\eta$. The four supported family--link combinations have losses
\begin{equation}
\ell(y,\beta_0,\beta,X)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n
\begin{cases}
\tfrac12(y_i-\eta_i)^2, & \text{Gaussian, identity link},\\
\log(1+e^{\eta_i})-y_i\eta_i, & \text{Bernoulli, logit link},\\
e^{\eta_i}-y_i\eta_i, & \text{Poisson, log link},\\
y_i e^{-\eta_i}+\eta_i, & \text{Gamma, log link}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
Constants independent of the parameters are omitted, and the Gamma shape is fixed at one for the loss normalization. The first three links are canonical; the Gamma log link is not canonical but still yields a convex loss for positive responses. All losses are averaged over the training observations. The intercept is fitted by default and is excluded from both coefficient and contribution penalties.

\subsection{Contribution Caps}

For evaluation observation $i$ and feature $j$, the realized contribution to the linear predictor is $Z_{ij}\beta_j$. A contribution ceiling $L_j\geq0$ specifies the desired upper limit on its magnitude. The target inequality $|Z_{ij}\beta_j|\leq L_j$ is encouraged by a penalty rather than imposed as a hard constraint.

The evaluation matrix $Z\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}$ has the same feature columns and units as $X$ but may contain different observations. Setting $Z=X$ assesses training contributions; other choices include specified stress scenarios and observed covariates at prediction time. In a sequential forecast, the current covariates may enter the penalty before their response is known, provided they are available when the forecast is issued. Figure~\ref{fig:timing} summarizes this information flow.

This separation matters when the behaviors of concern are poorly represented by average training observations. A risk model might include a set of specified adverse covariate combinations in $Z$, while a sequential prediction system can assess the contribution of the next observed covariate row before its outcome arrives. The likelihood continues to use the historical $(X,y)$; the rows of $Z$ affect coefficients only through the cap penalty. Choosing scenarios does not make the fitted rule automatically robust to every future value, and using a covariate row in $Z$ is legitimate for a forecast only when that row would be available at the stated forecast origin.

An optional row-wise multiplier $w_i>0$ rescales contributions for cap assessment. It is supplied through the \code{multiplier} argument and does not enter the likelihood. For example, it can represent an observation-specific exposure scale. The resulting penalty is
\begin{equation}
C(\beta,Z,L,w)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^p\sum_{i=1}^m
\left[\frac{(|w_iZ_{ij}\beta_j|-L_j)_+}{w_i}\right]^2,
\qquad (a)_+=\max(a,0).
\label{eq:weighted-cap}
\end{equation}
The normalization by $w_i$ makes this equivalent to assessing unscaled contributions against row-specific ceilings $L_j/w_i$. With the default $w_i=1$, it reduces to
\begin{equation}
C(\beta,Z,L)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^p\sum_{i=1}^m
(|Z_{ij}\beta_j|-L_j)_+^2.
\label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
The penalty is zero within the ceilings and grows quadratically with exceedances. It is convex and continuously differentiable, including at the activation boundary.

Ceilings are expressed on the linear-predictor scale. In Gaussian regression, a feature contributes additively to the mean. In logistic regression, it contributes to log-odds; in Poisson and Gamma regression, it contributes to the log-mean. If a contribution satisfies its ceiling, its odds or mean multiplier lies within $[e^{-L_j},e^{L_j}]$ for the default $w_i=1$. A soft penalty encourages this range but does not guarantee it. Nor does a feature-level ceiling directly bound the total prediction or a feature's share of that prediction.

\subsection{Penalized Estimation}

The estimator minimizes a loss with separate coefficient and contribution penalties:
\begin{equation}
(\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta)=\arg\min_{\beta_0,\beta}
\left\{
\underbrace{\ell(y,\beta_0,\beta,X)}_{\text{predictive loss}}
+\underbrace{\lambda\!\left[\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\|\beta\|_2^2+\alpha\|\beta\|_1\right]}_{\text{elastic net penalty}}
+\underbrace{\gamma C(\beta,Z,L,w)}_{\text{contribution penalty}}
\right\}.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Here $\lambda\geq0$ controls coefficient regularization, $\alpha\in[0,1]$ determines the elastic net mixture, and $\gamma\geq0$ controls contribution penalization. This parameterization follows the elastic net convention of \citet{friedman2010}. The $\ell_1$ term encourages sparsity, the $\ell_2$ term stabilizes coefficients under collinearity, and the cap penalty discourages excessive contributions on $Z$. Setting $\gamma=0$ recovers an elastic net GLM; setting $\lambda=0$ leaves only contribution regularization.

These components serve different purposes and can be used together. A coefficient may be small in standardized units but still produce a large contribution when a feature takes an unusual value. Conversely, a coefficient that produces a large contribution on one selected row may improve fit enough to remain useful. Increasing $\gamma$ gives the cap penalty more weight relative to the loss and coefficient penalty; it does not turn a soft ceiling into a hard constraint. The meaningful range of $\gamma$ depends on the loss and the units of the predictors, so it must be interpreted together with the chosen family, $Z$, and $L$.

Equation~\ref{eq:loss} is stated on the original feature scale. By default, the implementation standardizes the training columns and applies the elastic net penalty to coefficients in those standardized units. The contribution penalty is always evaluated using original-scale $Z$ and coefficients. Thus, interpreting the coefficient penalty literally on the original scale requires \code{standardize = FALSE}; standardization changes its implied feature weights.

The objective is convex for the supported losses and fixed $Z$, $L$, and $w$. Its only nonsmooth component is the $\ell_1$ term. These properties permit limited-memory quasi-Newton optimization with specialized treatment of that term \citep{nocedal1980,andrew2007}; they do not guarantee that every numerical fit converges. Users should inspect the returned convergence status.

\subsection{Optimization}

For fits without finite coefficient bounds, \pkg{capnet} uses the \pkg{lbfgs} package \citep{lbfgs}. Standard L-BFGS is used when $\lambda\alpha=0$. When $\lambda\alpha>0$, the orthant-wise limited-memory quasi-Newton (OWL-QN) method handles the $\ell_1$ term \citep{andrew2007}. Fits with finite coefficient bounds instead use a projected proximal-gradient solver, with soft-thresholding for the $\ell_1$ term and projection onto the coefficient bounds.

Let $F$ denote the smooth part of Equation~\ref{eq:loss}. Its slope gradient is
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\beta F=\nabla_\beta\ell+\lambda(1-\alpha)\beta
+\gamma\nabla_\beta C.
\label{eq:gradient}
\end{equation}
The $\ell_1$ term is handled separately by the optimizer. For Gaussian, Bernoulli, and Poisson losses, the likelihood gradient is $X^\top(\mu-y)/n$. For the Gamma log-link loss, it is $X^\top(\bm{1}-y/\mu)/n$, where division is element-wise. The intercept gradient is the average of the corresponding residual expression.

The contribution-penalty gradient has components
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C}{\partial\beta_j}
=\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m
\frac{(|w_iZ_{ij}\beta_j|-L_j)_+}{w_i}
\operatorname{sign}(w_iZ_{ij}\beta_j)Z_{ij}.
\label{eq:cap-gradient}
\end{equation}
The implementation evaluates this gradient in matrix form:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\beta C=\frac{2}{m}\big((V\odot S)\odot Z\big)^\top\bm{1},
\label{eq:matrix-gradient}
\end{equation}
where $A=\operatorname{diag}(w)Z\operatorname{diag}(\beta)$,
$S=\operatorname{sign}(A)$, and
$V=\operatorname{diag}(w)^{-1}(|A|-\bm{1}L^\top)_+$.
Absolute values, signs, positive parts, and $\odot$ operate element-wise. With standardized training columns, the optimizer first maps slopes back to original units to evaluate contributions, then divides the resulting gradient component by the corresponding training-column scale using the chain rule.

For dense matrices, evaluating the cap penalty and its gradient costs $O(mp)$ per iteration. Changing $Z$ affects this computation but does not alter the likelihood data $(X,y)$.

This section illustrates model fitting, sequential prediction, and diagnostics. We begin with Gaussian regression and then show how the same interface supports other families. The package can be installed from its GitHub repository and loaded as follows:

\begin{Code}
    R> remotes::install_github("harryziyuhe/capnet")
    R> library("capnet")
\end{Code}

We generate a small simulated dataset containing a training design matrix, response vector, contribution ceilings, and evaluation matrix using \code{simulate_capnet_data()}. Printed coefficients and predictions below illustrate one simulation draw; fresh calls can produce different numerical values.

\begin{Code}
    R> data <- simulate_capnet_data(n=100, p=10, m=20)
    R> X <- data$X
    R> y <- data$y
    R> L <- data$L
    R> Z <- data$Z
\end{Code}

Table~\ref{tab:capnet-functions} summarizes the main functions. The estimation interface follows familiar conventions from \pkg{glmnet} \citep{friedman2010}. The \code{capnet()} function requires the design matrix \code{x}, response \code{y}, and ceiling vector \code{L}. Optional arguments include the evaluation matrix \code{z}; model \code{family}; penalty parameters \code{alpha}, \code{lambda}, and \code{gamma}; and coefficient bounds \code{lower.limits} and \code{upper.limits}.

By default, \code{capnet()} fits Gaussian regression with an intercept, uses the training matrix for cap assessment, and applies no penalties or finite coefficient bounds. The following examples add coefficient penalties, contribution penalties, a separate evaluation matrix, and coefficient bounds in turn.

\begin{Code}
    # Unpenalized Gaussian regression
    R> fit1 <- capnet(X, y, L)
    
    # Elastic net penalties without contribution regularization
    R> alpha <- 0.5
    R> lambda <- 0.1
    R> fit2 <- capnet(X, y, L, alpha = alpha, lambda = lambda)
    
    # Adding contribution cap penalties, evaluated on training design matrix
    R> gamma <- 1
    R> fit3 <- capnet(X, y, L, alpha = alpha, lambda = lambda, gamma = gamma)
    
    # Evaluating capnet on evaluation matrix
    R> fit4 <- capnet(X, y, L, z = Z, alpha = alpha, lambda = lambda,
    +    gamma = gamma)

    # Adding box constraints
    R> upper.limits <- c(rep(Inf, 5), rep(0, 5))
    R> lower.limits <- c(rep(0, 5), rep(-Inf, 5))
    R> fit5 <- capnet(X, y, L, z = Z, alpha = alpha, lambda = lambda,
    +    gamma = gamma, lower.limits = lower.limits, upper.limits = upper.limits)
\end{Code}

The function returns an \code{S3} object with \code{coef()} and \code{predict()} methods for extracting coefficients and predicting on new covariate data.

The \code{walk_capnet()} function re-optimizes the model over successive slices of the evaluation matrix. Within a call, the training data $(X,y)$ remain fixed; only the evaluation rows used by the contribution penalty change. The \code{walk} argument specifies the number of evaluation rows in each slice, with a default of one. For time-series forecasting, the user must update the historical training window externally and ensure that every covariate in a slice is available when its forecasts are issued. Local fitting may avoid imposing one compromise coefficient vector across diverse scenarios, at the cost of repeated optimization.

\section{Applications}\label{sec:examples}

\subsection{Quantitative Finance}\label{sec:examples-finance}

In market timing, return forecasts are often mapped into equity positions. A forecast dominated by one extreme feature can therefore make both the position's direction and its size depend heavily on that signal. If the feature is noisy or transient, a large forecast change can translate into a fragile decision. Portfolio managers commonly use position limits, exposure caps, and risk budgets to control such outcomes after the forecast is formed. Contribution penalties express a related preference within the forecasting model itself: they discourage one input from accounting for an unusually large part of the linear predictor while the coefficients are estimated. They complement downstream portfolio controls, but do not replace them or guarantee lower investment losses. The analysis below evaluates forecast accuracy and feature contributions rather than the performance of a specified trading strategy.

To illustrate the empirical performance of \pkg{capnet}, we use data from the \href{https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/hull-tactical-market-prediction}{Hull Tactical Market Prediction Kaggle competition}. This dataset contains daily observations combining publicly available market data with proprietary features designed to predict excess returns of the S\&P 500. Following the variable selection of the competition’s baseline model, we construct a backtesting pipeline to evaluate alternative approaches to imposing contribution caps within a linear modeling framework. For reproducibility, a copy of this dataset is bundled with the package as \code{inst/extdata/data.csv}, and the full backtesting pipeline used in this section is provided in \path{vignettes/paper/example_elan.Rmd}.

\centering

Table~\ref{tab:variable} summarizes selected covariates. Features $S5$ and $P12$ have substantial positive sample excess kurtosis, indicating occasional large deviations relative to their typical variation. That observation does not establish that every extreme value is unreliable. It does show why average covariate scales alone may miss the circumstances in which a forecast depends heavily on a particular feature. The backtest therefore examines realized contributions alongside forecast accuracy, rather than treating sample tail behavior as sufficient evidence of a predictive benefit from capping.

We consider three modeling approaches: (i) an uncapped baseline, (ii) a post-fit capping method, and (iii) the walk-forward \pkg{capnet} approach. At each monthly refit date, models are estimated using a 25-year rolling training window and used to generate predictions for the subsequent month. Given training data \code{x_train}, \code{y_train}, test data \code{x_test}, and a specified contribution cap vector \code{L}, we estimate \code{capnet} or \code{walk_capnet} models with \code{alpha = 0.5} and a regularization parameter \code{lambda} selected via \code{cv.glmnet}. For the post-fit capping approach, we first estimate the uncapped model (equivalently, \code{capnet} with \code{gamma = 0}) and then apply contribution caps to the fitted model \textit{ex post}. While this guarantees that realized contributions satisfy the cap constraint, it does not allow the model to adjust coefficients in response to the constraint during estimation. In contrast, \pkg{capnet} incorporates the cap penalty directly into the objective function, allowing the model to reallocate weight across features in a manner that balances predictive accuracy with contribution control. The backtest spans January 1, 2020 through February 13, 2026. For the walk-forward specification, we evaluate multiple values of the penalty parameter \code{gamma} to assess sensitivity to the strength of the cap penalty.

The supplied helper sets ceilings to $L_j=(7/350)|\operatorname{cor}(X_{\cdot j},y)|$, using complete training observations. This correlation-based heuristic differs from the univariate Poisson-slope rule used in the web application. Within each monthly training window, \code{cv.glmnet()} selects \code{lambda.min} using ten random folds; the selected $\lambda$ is shared across the contribution-penalty specifications. Thus, the outer monthly evaluation is chronological, while the inner tuning procedure is not a forward-chaining validation.

Both applications use the squared-error out-of-sample score implemented by \code{calc_oos_r2()}:
\begin{equation}
R^2_{\mathrm{OOS}}=1-
\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}(y_t-\hat y_t)^2}
{\sum_{t=2}^{T}(y_t-\bar y_{t-1})^2},
\qquad \bar y_{t-1}=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}y_s.
\label{eq:oos-r2}
\end{equation}
Here $t$ indexes ordered evaluation observations. The benchmark mean uses preceding responses within the evaluation period, rather than the initial training sample, and the first evaluation observation is omitted from scoring. The table reports one pooled score per specification, not an average of monthly $R^2$ values. Rolling scores restrict the two error sums to the displayed window while retaining the expanding benchmark. Table~\ref{tab:elan_result} reports the finance results.

\subsection{Web Traffic}\label{sec:examples-web}

The second application forecasts aggregate web traffic from article-level pageview series. Pageviews can change abruptly after a news event, a viral post, or a shift in public interest. Such episodes create extreme predictor values that may be poorly represented in a rolling training window. In a Poisson log-link model, an individual contribution enters the log-mean: adding a contribution of $c$ multiplies the conditional mean by $e^c$ if all other terms are held fixed. A large positive contribution can therefore amplify a predicted count substantially, and even a coefficient that appears modest on ordinary days can matter greatly on a high-traffic day.

To illustrate this, we use daily English Wikipedia pageviews (January 1, 2022 through December 31, 2024) for 30 widely-read articles spanning major technology companies, artificial-intelligence and cryptocurrency topics, and prominent public figures, obtained from the public Wikimedia Pageviews REST API. The response is next-day total pageviews summed across all 30 articles; the covariates are each article's own pageviews (in thousands of views) on the current day. Table~\ref{tab:wiki_variable} reports summary statistics for a representative subset of the covariate panel. Several articles exhibit large positive sample excess kurtosis. In particular, \code{Microsoft} and \code{YouTube} have much larger values than the finance covariates in Table~\ref{tab:variable}. The processed panel is bundled as \path{inst/extdata/wiki_traffic.csv}. The reproducible backtest is in \path{vignettes/paper/example_wiki.Rmd}.

It remains conditional on five-row covariate batches, and the full per-specification scores are exported alongside the full-period results.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduces \pkg{capnet}, an \proglang{R} package for fitting generalized linear models with contribution-capped regularization. The proposed framework extends classical penalized regression methods by directly constraining the realized contributions of covariates to the linear predictor. By shifting the focus of regularization from coefficients to feature-level influence, \pkg{capnet} addresses a class of modeling problems in which extreme covariate realizations can disproportionately affect predictions.

The contribution-cap formulation integrates naturally with generalized linear models and yields a convex optimization problem that can be efficiently solved using limited-memory quasi-Newton methods. The ability to specify an evaluation matrix distinct from the training data further enhances flexibility, allowing users to enforce stability not only in-sample but also in anticipated stress scenarios. The package provides a practical implementation of this framework, including tools for model fitting, cross-validation, sequential estimation, and diagnostic analysis, with an interface designed to be consistent with established workflows.

Empirical results across two distinct domains---a Gaussian quantitative finance application and a Poisson web-traffic forecasting application---demonstrate that contribution-based regularization can improve predictive stability and performance in settings with heavy-tailed covariates. In particular, \pkg{capnet} reduces the dominance of individual covariates by redistributing predictive weight across features, leading to smoother out-of-sample performance and fewer extreme deviations. This benefit was most dramatic in the web-traffic application, where an uncapped model's forecast was occasionally dominated by a single viral covariate to catastrophic effect, but persisted even after removing that extreme scenario, indicating that the gains from \pkg{capnet} are not solely an artifact of avoiding rare events. Compared to post-hoc capping approaches, which enforce constraints after estimation, \pkg{capnet} incorporates these constraints directly into the optimization problem, resulting in a more coherent and effective trade-off between robustness and predictive accuracy.

Several limitations and directions for future research remain. The selection of contribution caps requires domain knowledge or heuristic procedures. Developing principled, data-driven approaches for tuning these parameters is an important area for further work. In addition, while the current implementation scales well to moderate evaluation sets, extending the framework to very large or streaming evaluation matrices may require additional computational strategies. Finally, further exploration of the relationship between contribution-based regularization and other robustness frameworks, such as distributionally robust optimization, may yield useful theoretical and practical insights.

Overall, \pkg{capnet} expands the toolkit of penalized regression by introducing a new dimension of control over model behavior. By aligning statistical estimation with practical constraints on covariate influence, it provides a flexible and interpretable framework for applications in which stability, robustness, and feature-level interpretability are of central importance.
